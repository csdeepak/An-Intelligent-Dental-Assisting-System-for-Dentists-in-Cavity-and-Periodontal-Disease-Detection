\begin{abstract}
Automated screening of panoramic radiographs is almost always reported with
tooth-level metrics: which teeth are carious. Such numbers are easy to compute,
easy to compare, and---we argue---remarkably hard to interpret, because the
literature rarely establishes what an uninformed system would score on the same
images. We built a six-stage panoramic pipeline, validated it on four external
hospitals, calibrated its confidences and gave it a principled way to decline,
and then set out to answer a narrower question: what does its caries number
actually mean? To find out, we scored it against progressively less informed
opponents under one identical scorer---a 33\,B-parameter vision--language
model, an 8\,B one, and finally a baseline that is not shown the radiograph at
all. The comparison did not resolve the way we expected, and it did not resolve
the same way inside and outside our development data. We report what separates
a diagnostic system from an anatomical prior on this task, what does not, and
which of our own results survive the distinction.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
panoramic radiography, dental caries, evaluation methodology, baselines,
external validation, model calibration, selective prediction
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
%% =====================================================================

Consider a screening report. Against a panoramic radiograph it lists four
tooth codes---16, 26, 36, 46---and marks them carious. A clinician reading that
report has a reasonable first impression: the system has looked at the image and
found something. The codes are specific. They are plausible. They are, often
enough, correct.

Now consider a second program. It emits the same four codes for every
radiograph it is ever shown. It contains no network, no features, no pixels---
only a list. On a large enough set of panoramic images, it too will be right
often enough to produce a respectable-looking number.

The distance between those two programs is the subject of this paper.

Panoramic radiographs (OPGs) are the most common extraoral radiograph in
dentistry, and automated caries and periodontal screening on them is an active
field~\cite{lim2024dual,chen2024cariesxrays,bilal2024pdcnet}. Reported
tooth-level accuracies cluster in a range that reads as clinically useful. What
is much rarer is a statement of the floor: what the same metric would return for
a system that does not perceive anything at all.

Two properties of this task make that floor unusually high, and both are
anatomical rather than algorithmic. First, caries is not uniformly distributed
across the dentition---it concentrates heavily in molars. Second, tooth-level
metrics ask a \emph{set-membership} question: which teeth, from a fixed
vocabulary of 32 codes. A question of that shape can be answered from anatomy
alone, without reference to any particular patient. Together these mean that a
fixed answer inherits a substantial score by construction.

The natural response is to check. We surveyed 49 task-relevant papers from our
own review of the area; \textbf{two} report validation on data from an
institution other than the one that produced their training set, and
\textbf{none} report a constant, image-blind, or otherwise uninformed baseline.
The floor is, in this literature, essentially unmeasured.

This paper is our attempt to measure it on our own work rather than anyone
else's. We first built a system worth testing---a six-stage pipeline performing
tooth detection, FDI numbering, caries classification and periodontal bone-loss
classification, evaluated with cluster-bootstrapped confidence intervals,
calibrated probabilities, a pre-registered abstention threshold, and external
validation across four hospitals it never trained on. We then constructed a
ladder of opponents, each given strictly less information than the last, and
scored every rung with one scorer on one set of images.

We report the outcome in full, including the parts that do not favour our
system, and including a disagreement between our development data and our
external data that we did not anticipate and cannot yet explain. Our
contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{constant-baseline protocol} for tooth-level OPG evaluation,
        together with what it reveals when applied across two unrelated metric
        families and five hospitals.
  \item A \textbf{vision--language-model baseline with the controls that make
        it interpretable}---including what these models answer when shown an
        image containing no anatomy whatsoever.
  \item \textbf{External validation on four hospitals}, reported per site,
        with the spread that pooling conceals.
  \item \textbf{Calibration and selective prediction} results, each with a
        control isolating the mechanism responsible.
  \item An honest characterisation of the pipeline itself, including one
        branch that does not survive domain shift and a headline number that
        is bounded by an earlier stage.
\end{enumerate}

We are explicit about what we do not claim. We do not assert that published
caries results are incorrect. We argue something narrower and, we think, more
useful: that a tooth-level number reported without a floor cannot be
distinguished from an anatomical prior---a statement about \emph{evidence},
not about anyone's system.

\section{Related Work}
%% =====================================================================

\subsection{Detection and classification on panoramic radiographs}
Deep learning on OPGs has converged on a familiar decomposition: detect teeth,
then classify each one. Detector choice is well studied, and recent head-to-head
evaluation on dental panoramics supports YOLOv11 over its successor generation
for multi-class dental detection~\cite{asif2026yolo26,nassiri2025yolo}. The same
body of work consistently reports an asymmetry that matters here: performance is
strong on well-defined objects (crowns, implants, restorations) and markedly
weaker on subtle radiolucent pathology such as caries and bone
loss~\cite{chen2023deep,alharbi2023detection}. Caries segmentation and
classification specifically have been approached with dual-network
frameworks~\cite{lim2024dual}, attention architectures~\cite{bilal2024pdcnet},
and cascaded designs~\cite{chen2024cariesxrays}.

\subsection{Tooth numbering}
FDI numbering is typically reported at very high accuracy---frequently above
0.97---but often on \emph{pre-cropped} teeth, where the task is a 32-way
classification of an image that is already known to contain exactly one tooth,
correctly framed~\cite{ayhan2025detection,karamuftuo2025panoramic}. This is a
materially easier problem than numbering under detector output, where teeth may
be missed, duplicated or clipped. We report both framings and find the gap
between them large.

\subsection{Vision--language models in dentistry}
Recent work applies general-purpose and domain-adapted VLMs to dental
imaging~\cite{meng2026dentvlm,jha2026dentiask,li2026pandent,hao2025mmoral}.
These systems are attractive because they need no task-specific training, and
are difficult to evaluate for the same reason: a fluent, confident, correctly
formatted answer is not evidence of perception. We adopt a control from the
broader shortcut-learning literature~\cite{elkhoury2019geffner}: present the
model with an input containing none of the signal it purports to read, and
observe whether its behaviour changes.

\subsection{External validation and the missing floor}
External validation remains uncommon. In our review of 49 task-relevant papers,
two evaluate on an outside institution. Where it is done, degradation is often
severe---one reported system falls from 95.06 mAP on its own data to 61.83 on a
public benchmark. Independent work on periodontal bone loss reports weighted F1
of 0.29 with severe-class F1 of zero against clinician
reference~\cite{ren2026dualbranch,george2026explainable}, a reminder that
in-domain figures can be a poor guide to behaviour elsewhere. To our knowledge,
no work in this area reports an image-blind baseline, which is the gap this
paper addresses.

\section{System}
%% =====================================================================

The finding in this paper is only interesting if the system it is measured
against is a serious one. We therefore describe the pipeline before the
evaluation that tests it.

\subsection{Architecture}
The pipeline maps an OPG to a structured per-tooth record in six stages.
\textbf{Stage 1} detects teeth with a YOLOv11l segmentation
model~\cite{jocher2024yolo11}. \textbf{Stage 2} assigns each detection a
two-digit FDI code using a ResNet18~\cite{he2016resnet} head that consumes the
tooth crop together with four positional features, resolved to a one-to-one
assignment across the mouth by the Hungarian algorithm~\cite{kuhn1955hungarian}.
\textbf{Stages 3 and 4} classify caries and periodontal bone loss per tooth
using independent ResNet18 branches, each arranged as a coarse-to-fine cascade.
\textbf{Stages 5 and 6} fuse the per-stage outputs on the FDI key and emit the
record.

The whole system totals approximately 25\,M parameters and runs at
2.39\,s per image on CPU with no GPU present. This figure becomes relevant in
Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Data}
Tooth detection and bone loss use the Tufts Dental
Database~\cite{panetta2021tufts} (1{,}000 OPGs) under a frozen
700/150/150 split. Caries uses DENTEX~\cite{hamamci2023dentex}. FDI numbering
trains on both, with a third public corpus used for an ablation.

All splits are \textbf{image-level}. Two crops from one radiograph are not
independent observations, and a tooth-level split would place the same mouth in
both training and test data.

Two labelling conventions were established by measurement rather than assumed,
because a silent error in either would have trained a plausible but wrong model.
Tufts numbering is left--right \textbf{mirrored} relative to Universal notation
(Tufts ``1'' corresponds to FDI 28, not 18), confirmed three independent ways.
The third corpus was checked the same way: using the two-corpus numbering head
as an independent arbiter over 915 teeth, agreement was \textbf{80.3\,\%}
reading its labels as written against \textbf{1.7\,\%} reading them mirrored.

\subsection{External data}
Four hospitals from an independent Chinese cohort (denoted test1--test4) serve
as locked external test data: never trained on, never tuned against, and
evaluated a bounded and recorded number of times.

\subsection{Crop geometry and pre-registration}
Classifier inputs are expanded crops resized to $224\times224$. Expansion is
fixed per branch and identical across corpora---symmetric $1.5\times$ for
numbering, $1.5\times$ width by $2.0\times$ height for bone loss, since teeth
are roughly three times taller than wide and horizontal over-expansion bleeds
into neighbouring teeth.

Operating points are fixed \emph{a priori} and reported where they fall rather
than tuned against evaluation data. One consequence is reported here rather
than omitted: the detector's mask-quality bar was pre-registered at 0.65 and
\textbf{missed}, at 0.638. The bar was not moved after the fact; the model was
adopted for its bounding boxes alone by an explicit, dated decision, and the
mask-dependent component it was intended to enable remains unwired.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
%% =====================================================================

Having built the system, we wanted to know what its caries number meant. The
protocol below is the paper's methodological core.

\subsection{The ladder}
Four systems answer one question---\emph{which teeth in this radiograph show
caries?}---on the same 164 leakage-free DENTEX images, scored by one scorer per
metric family. Each rung is given strictly less information than the one above.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Pipeline}: the six stages of Section~III.
  \item \textbf{VLM (33\,B)}: Qwen2.5-VL-33B-Instruct~\cite{bai2025qwen25vl},
        zero-shot.
  \item \textbf{VLM (8\,B)}: the same family at 8\,B.
  \item \textbf{Constant}: a fixed set of FDI codes emitted for every image.
        \textbf{The radiograph is never read.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Fairness}
A rigged baseline is worse than no baseline. The VLMs are given the FDI
numbering scheme in the prompt, told explicitly that teeth may be absent, and
parsed permissively, so that neither notation nor formatting is scored as
clinical error. Our pipeline receives no corresponding assistance. Every
affordance favours the opponent.

The constant is fitted greedily on 78 leakage-excluded images that are disjoint
from the scored set---overlap asserted to be zero at runtime---and then frozen.
We also report a variant fitted directly on the test set; it is labelled an
oracle throughout and is not claimable.

\subsection{Metrics}
We use two metric families, chosen to fail differently.

\textbf{FDI-set F1} treats each image as a set-prediction problem: micro-averaged
precision, recall and F1 over predicted versus annotated FDI codes.

\textbf{Lesion-localisation agreement} asks, of every expert-annotated lesion,
whether a tooth was flagged that spatially overlaps it. It requires spatial
correspondence, which set membership does not. Because it has no negative class
by construction---a deliberate property, since our external cohort lacks healthy
controls---it is a recall-style measure, and flagging more teeth can only raise
it. We therefore always report it alongside the predicted-positive match rate
and the number of teeth flagged, and we include an upper-bound arm that flags
\emph{every} detected tooth.

\subsection{Controls}
For the VLMs, we present a \textbf{blank grey image} and a
\textbf{random-noise image} under the identical prompt. Neither contains a
tooth. For selective prediction, we \textbf{shuffle the confidences}, breaking
the link between confidence and correctness while preserving both marginals; a
gain that survives shuffling is an artefact of discarding items rather than of
ranking them.

\textbf{The development-set result does not replicate.} Pooled, the difference
is $-0.89$ percentage points with 95\,\% CI $[-4.88, +3.01]$: the two systems
are statistically indistinguishable, with the pipeline nominally ahead by an
amount that is indistinguishable from noise.

The per-site spread is the more useful observation. The difference swings from
$+15.8$ points at test1 to $-10.6$ at test2---a 26-point range across four
hospitals of a single cohort---and the pooled figure conceals this entirely.
Notably, test1 is simultaneously the pipeline's worst site and the constant's
best. We do not have an explanation for that, and we report it unexplained
rather than omit it.

Across all five hospitals, then, the defensible claim is \emph{non-separability}:
a purpose-built pipeline and an image-blind prior cannot be reliably told apart
by these metrics. That is weaker than the development-set result alone would
support, and it is the claim the full evidence sustains.

\subsection{What the constant cannot do}

A constant answers a set-membership question. It cannot perform tasks whose
output is not a fixed list, and the pipeline's results on those tasks are
correspondingly meaningful.

\textbf{Tooth numbering.} End-to-end FDI-exact accuracy is 58.3\,\%
($546/936$ teeth), 95\,\% CI $[53.9, 62.8]$. This figure is
\textbf{detection-bound} and we report it as two numbers: 29.4\,\% of
ground-truth teeth are never detected, and of the 661 teeth Stage 1 does find,
Stage 2 numbers \textbf{82.6\,\%} correctly. Residual errors are largely benign:
of 115 incorrect codes, 89 (77.4\,\%) are off-by-one within the correct
quadrant, and only 16 of 661 detected teeth (2.4\,\%) receive a quadrant-level
error. A constant cannot attempt this task at all.

\textbf{Calibration.} The caries gate's confidences are not probabilities:
expected calibration error is 0.136 and the model is systematically
overconfident. Temperature scaling~\cite{guo2017calibration} made calibration
\emph{worse} (0.152), indicating the miscalibration is structural rather than a
single scale factor; isotonic regression~\cite{zadrozny2002isotonic} reduced ECE
to \textbf{0.043}, a 68\,\% improvement, and is applied to confidences only,
leaving every verdict byte-identical.

\textbf{Selective prediction.} Declining the least-confident quarter of cases
raises numbering accuracy from 0.826 to \textbf{0.885}
($+5.91$ pp, CI $[+3.88, +7.68]$) and caries-gate accuracy from 0.797 to
\textbf{0.940} ($+14.25$ pp, CI $[+11.32, +16.63]$). Under the shuffle control
both gains collapse to $+0.03$ and $+0.00$ percentage points respectively,
establishing that the improvement comes from the confidence ranking and not from
discarding items. The pre-registered threshold of 0.30 lands at 90.8\,\%
coverage and 0.847 accuracy, within 0.2 points of the empirical 90\,\%-coverage
operating point---a value chosen before these data were seen.

One caveat must accompany any coverage figure here: coverage is measured over
\emph{detected} teeth. Retaining 75\,\% of detections corresponds to 75\,\% of
70.6\,\%, or 53.0\,\% of the teeth actually present.

\subsection{Vision--language model behaviour}

Few-shot prompting, with exemplars drawn strictly from leakage-excluded images,
degrades the 8\,B model monotonically: F1 falls from 0.149 zero-shot to 0.066 at
two shots and 0.034 at four. The mechanism is abstention rather than error---
empty answers rise from 56 of 164 images to 123 of 164---so exact-set match
simultaneously \emph{rises}, because 16 images contain no caries and an empty
answer scores an exact match on each. Reported alone, that column would be true
and thoroughly misleading.

The control is more direct. Presented with a \textbf{blank grey image} and with
\textbf{random noise}, both models return confident, correctly formatted,
dental-specific answers; for one model the response to pure noise is identical
to its response to a real radiograph. Whatever these outputs measure, it is not
perception of the image in front of them.

The efficiency comparison survives all of the above and is worth stating
plainly: 33\,B parameters and 70\,GB of accelerator memory are outperformed by
approximately 25\,M parameters running on a CPU.

\subsection{Where the pipeline fails}

The bone-loss branch reaches 0.923 macro-F1 in-domain on 1{,}558 validation
crops at an untouched 4\,\% prevalence (sensitivity 83.05\,\%, specificity
99.53\,\%) and \textbf{does not survive domain shift}, firing on 0.7\,\% of
DENTEX teeth and 2.4\,\% of external teeth. A detector change that moved
numbering by $+12.0$ points and caries agreement by $+7.7$ moved bone loss by
nothing, which falsifies the hypothesis that a shared upstream cause explains
all three. We report this as a measured negative result rather than a component.

\section{Discussion}
%% =====================================================================

\subsection{Why the floor is high}
Caries in these cohorts is anatomically concentrated in molars. A tooth-level
decision is a set-membership question, and set membership inherits that base
rate directly. The consequence is that headroom above a fixed anatomical answer
is small---and, importantly, small \emph{independently of model quality}. This
is a property of the task--metric pairing, not of any particular system.

That two unrelated metrics agree on development data matters. Set-membership F1
and lesion localisation were designed to measure different things; the second was
adopted specifically because it requires spatial correspondence. A single-metric
result would be a metric artefact. Two are a property of the data.

\subsection{Why the external disagreement matters more}
We expected the external cohort to confirm the development finding. It did not:
pooled, the systems are indistinguishable, and per site the difference swings
through 26 points. Two readings are available and we cannot presently separate
them. Either the development result is partly a property of DENTEX's particular
caries distribution, or the external metric---which necessarily uses centroid
containment rather than overlap, because external lesions are small focal blobs
inside much larger tooth boxes---has different sensitivity. Both are consistent
with the data we have.

What the disagreement does establish is that a \emph{single} external number is
not informative at this variance. Our own pooled figure of 0.408 is, on its own,
as uninformative as any other pooled figure in this literature. We report per
site for that reason, and recommend the same of others.

\subsection{What should be reported}
We propose that a constant-baseline row accompany any tooth-level caries metric
on this class of data. It costs one line of code and no compute. Without it, a
mid-range tooth-level number cannot be interpreted---as our four systems
demonstrate, spanning 0.149 to 0.320 with the \emph{least} informed entry at the
top.

This does not invalidate prior work. It means one specific claim---\emph{this
system perceives caries}---is not established by a tooth-level number alone.

\subsection{What still separates a system from a prior}
A constant cannot number teeth, cannot produce a calibrated confidence, cannot
abstain informatively, and has no behaviour under domain shift to measure. We
propose these as the axes on which OPG systems should be compared:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Identification}: can it say \emph{which} tooth?
        (82.6\,\% of detected teeth here.)
  \item \textbf{Calibration}: are its confidences probabilities?
        (ECE 0.136 $\rightarrow$ 0.043.)
  \item \textbf{Selective prediction}: does declining improve accuracy, and does
        the gain survive a shuffle control? ($+14.25$ pp; yes.)
  \item \textbf{Domain shift}: measured on held-out institutions and reported
        per site.
\end{enumerate}

Our pipeline performs meaningfully on all four. Its caries F1 conveys much less.

\subsection{On reporting negative results}
Two findings here are unfavourable to our own system: the constant comparison,
and a bone-loss branch that does not generalise. We report both, because a
screening tool that fails silently is more dangerous than one that fails
visibly---and because the alternative, selecting whichever metric flatters the
system, is precisely the practice this paper argues against.

%% =====================================================================
\section{Limitations}
%% =====================================================================

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Two caries cohorts, disagreeing in direction.} The constant
        leads on development data and ties externally. We report both rather
        than the more striking one; the common finding is non-separability.
  \item \textbf{No clinician comparison.} We do not know how any system here
        compares against a dentist on these images. This is the single largest
        gap and is the subject of ongoing work.
  \item \textbf{Tooth-level is our finest localisation.} The localisation metric
        is a tooth-level correspondence check, not a pixel-level segmentation
        assessment.
  \item \textbf{One VLM family} at two scales; a domain-adapted medical VLM was
        not accessible.
  \item \textbf{Detection bounds Stage 2.} At 0.810 box mAP@0.5, 29.4\,\% of
        teeth are never presented to the numbering head.
  \item \textbf{The external cohort lacks healthy controls}, so specificity and
        AUC are not computable there; we report lesion agreement only.
\end{enumerate}

%% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%% =====================================================================

We set out to determine what a tooth-level caries number means, using our own
system as the subject. On development data, a baseline that never reads the
radiograph outscored a purpose-built pipeline and two vision--language models
under two unrelated metrics. Across four external hospitals the same comparison
produced no separation at all, with a 26-point swing between sites.

The conclusion we draw is not that automated screening does not work. It is that
this measurement cannot tell us whether it does. Identification, calibration,
selective prediction and behaviour under domain shift can---and on those axes,
reported here with their controls, our pipeline has something to show. We
recommend that tooth-level caries results be accompanied by an image-blind
baseline and reported per institution, so that the next number of this kind is
interpretable when it appears.
